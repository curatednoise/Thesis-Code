Abstract

This thesis investigates the structural, material, and formal mechanisms required for Ivorian fashion to establish an autonomous design identity and function as a vehicle of cultural soft power. This study analyses how contemporary Ivorian Couture re-centers traditional West African textile lineages into a sovereign language of modern luxury.

The research demonstrates that sartorial sovereignty relies on combining domestic material heritage with complete ownership over the means of production. Through field observations of Abidjan’s hyper-decentralized retail ecosystem, the study highlights how local artisan networks provide an existing baseline for scalable, high-end production.

This material reality is evaluated alongside empirical visual data: a quantitative analysis of 177 womenswear looks from Abidjan Fashion Week 2025, which identifies a dominant regional design language defined by structural power cuts, fluid fabrics and heavy material grounding. Case studies illustrate how custom tailored garments serve as dynamic transport systems for national identity on global stages.

To bridge practice and policy, the thesis introduces a tripartite speculative design matrix mapping Ivorian sartorial authority across three geographic and formal registers: The Skyline (administrative precision and structured prêt-à-porter), The Ground (ancestral material sovereignty and zero-waste Akan geometry), and The Ocean (avant-garde fluid drapes and high-tech material innovation). Ultimately, the study concludes that while Ivorian couture possesses an undeniable aesthetic vocabulary, transforming local sartorial capital into an enduring engine of soft power requires sovereign trade infrastructure, intellectual property protections, and targeted state investment to retain cultural and economic value at home.

Keywords: Ivorian Couture, Soft Power, Sartorial Autonomy, Akan-Baoulé Textiles, Tapa Bark-Cloth, Abidjan Fashion Ecosystem, Fanon, Autonomous Design, Zero-Waste Geometry.

01

Research Question & Vantage Point

This thesis is about Ivorian couture. Before turning to it, it is worth situating the vantage point it is written from.

As a researcher of Franco-Danish heritage and Afro-European identity, educated within Western European design institutions, I do not stand outside the histories examined here; my own gaze is mediated by the very structures of validation this thesis interrogates. I arrive at Abidjan not as a neutral observer, but as a contemporary iteration of the diaspora traveler whose aesthetic expectations have been shaped by Western norms of legibility. The observation that first made this dynamic visible to me, however, took place not in Ivory Coast but in Vietnam, where I lived from 2024 to 2025.

That period in Vietnam revealed the extent to which culture functions as an active, defensive form of infrastructure. Cultural continuity was visibly maintained through language, food, ritual, and public symbolism, producing a resilient collective identity. This coherence appeared inseparable from Vietnam's history of successive resistance. Rather than treating culture as a secondary, decorative, or merely symbolic domain, this experience demonstrated that cultural cohesion operates as a primary strategic resource.

Primary Inquiry
How can Ivory Coast turn local couture into an autonomous engine of soft power?

Yet, when confronting my memory of Abidjan—Ivory Coast's largest city and de facto economic and cultural centre—this loop of cultural validation was not absent so much as short-circuited by the symbol meant to carry it. The Ivorian flag is omnipresent in Abidjan but it follows the exact three-band vertical structure of the French tricolor. A national symbol built on the coloniser's own template cannot generate the same legible, sovereign shorthand as flags originated from independence movements.

Note 1.1: Comparative Semiotics
The desaturation test between the French and Ivorian flags renders them visually indistinguishable, illustrating template reliance.


Note 1.2: Comparative Lens
Vietnam serves purely as a comparative analytical lens on sovereign self-assertion, not as a core subject of study.

Research Focus & Core Pillars

This research investigates Ivorian couture: how it can function as cultural infrastructure, shaping international perception, aspiration, and cultural legitimacy, and what it would take for a strong, recognisable Ivorian aesthetic identity to operate as an autonomous engine of soft power.

Pillar Framework Theoretical Core Application Scope
01. Theoretical & Psychological Fanon's 3-Phase Framework & Amin's Eurocentric Ideology Deconstructing colonial assimilation & reclaiming historical continuity (Akan-Baoulé).
02. Sartorial Subversion La Sape to Imane Ayissi's Grande Couture Grassroots subversion to high-fashion blueprints for Ivorian couture autonomy.
03. Political Economy Ha-Joon Chang's Dependency Model Overcoming raw material export dependencies via digital/spatial platforms.

The research proposes a speculative "Ivorian fashion identity"—a deliberately constructed aesthetic language, argued from within one Francophone West African context shaped by French colonial assimilation policy. Proposing this identity gives Ivorian couture a legible, coherent language to circulate on, rather than leaving that language undefined.

Structural Risk:
Local production faces a system positioned to export raw material rather than finished, high-margin goods.

Stakeholder Systems & Constraints

The stakeholder system operating around Ivorian fashion comprises three distinct structural rings:

Core (Cultural Production System): Independent designers, couturiers de famille, neighbourhood tailoring guilds, and material artisans (Tapa bark-cloth harvesters, Kente and Baoulé weavers) sit at the actual centre of this ecosystem. They decide what reads as desirable and contemporary without waiting on institutional direction.

Direct (Circulation Ecosystem): Domestic consumers, diaspora, high-visibility cultural ambassadors (e.g., national football team tarmac looks), and local platforms like Abidjan Fashion Week.

Indirect (Institutional Frameworks): State institutions including the Ministry of Culture, INSAAC, and UNESCO partnerships. Their involvement to date has been reactive and event-based rather than architectural.

Core Constraints:
• Institutional Risks: Lack of centralized policy or domestic protectionism.
• Material Value-Chain Risks: High labor times vs. fast commercial imports.
• Interpretive Risks: Binary trap between rigid "authenticity" and Eurocentric baselines.
