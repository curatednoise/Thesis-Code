01

Research Question & Vantage Point

This thesis is about Ivorian couture. Before turning to it, it is worth situating the vantage point it is written from.

As a researcher of Franco-Danish heritage and Afro-European identity, educated within Western European design institutions, I do not stand outside the histories examined here; my own gaze is mediated by the very structures of validation this thesis interrogates[cite: 1]. I arrive at Abidjan not as a neutral observer, but as a contemporary iteration of the diaspora traveler whose aesthetic expectations have been shaped by Western norms of legibility[cite: 1]. The observation that first made this dynamic visible to me, however, took place not in Ivory Coast but in Vietnam, where I lived from 2024 to 2025[cite: 1].

That period in Vietnam revealed the extent to which culture functions as an active, defensive form of infrastructure[cite: 1]. Cultural continuity was visibly maintained through language, food, ritual, and public symbolism, producing a resilient collective identity[cite: 1]. This coherence appeared inseparable from Vietnam's history of successive resistance[cite: 1]. Rather than treating culture as a secondary, decorative, or merely symbolic domain, this experience demonstrated that cultural cohesion operates as a primary strategic resource[cite: 1].

Primary Inquiry
How can Ivory Coast turn local couture into an autonomous engine of soft power?[cite: 1]

Yet, when confronting my memory of Abidjan—Ivory Coast's largest city and de facto economic and cultural centre—this loop of cultural validation was not absent so much as short-circuited by the symbol meant to carry it[cite: 1]. The Ivorian flag is omnipresent in Abidjan but it follows the exact three-band vertical structure of the French tricolor[cite: 1]. A national symbol built on the coloniser's own template cannot generate the same legible, sovereign shorthand as flags originated from independence movements[cite: 1].

Note 1.1: Comparative Semiotics
The desaturation test between the French and Ivorian flags renders them visually indistinguishable, illustrating template reliance[cite: 1].


Note 1.2: Comparative Lens
Vietnam serves purely as a comparative analytical lens on sovereign self-assertion, not as a core subject of study[cite: 1].

02

Research Focus & Core Pillars

This research investigates Ivorian couture: how it can function as cultural infrastructure, shaping international perception, aspiration, and cultural legitimacy, and what it would take for a strong, recognisable Ivorian aesthetic identity to operate as an autonomous engine of soft power[cite: 1].

Pillar Framework Theoretical Core Application Scope
01. Theoretical & Psychological Fanon's 3-Phase Framework & Amin's Eurocentric Ideology[cite: 1] Deconstructing colonial assimilation & reclaiming historical continuity (Akan-Baoulé)[cite: 1].
02. Sartorial Subversion La Sape to Imane Ayissi's Grande Couture[cite: 1] Grassroots subversion to high-fashion blueprints for Ivorian couture autonomy[cite: 1].
03. Political Economy Ha-Joon Chang's Dependency Model[cite: 1] Overcoming raw material export dependencies via digital/spatial platforms[cite: 1].

The research proposes a speculative "Ivorian fashion identity"—a deliberately constructed aesthetic language, argued from within one Francophone West African context shaped by French colonial assimilation policy[cite: 1]. Proposing this identity gives Ivorian couture a legible, coherent language to circulate on, rather than leaving that language undefined[cite: 1].

Structural Risk:
Local production faces a system positioned to export raw material rather than finished, high-margin goods[cite: 1].

Stakeholder Systems & Constraints

The stakeholder system operating around Ivorian fashion comprises three distinct structural rings[cite: 1]:

Core (Cultural Production System): Independent designers, couturiers de famille, neighbourhood tailoring guilds, and material artisans (Tapa bark-cloth harvesters, Kente and Baoulé weavers) sit at the actual centre of this ecosystem[cite: 1]. They decide what reads as desirable and contemporary without waiting on institutional direction[cite: 1].

Direct (Circulation Ecosystem): Domestic consumers, diaspora, high-visibility cultural ambassadors (e.g., national football team tarmac looks), and local platforms like Abidjan Fashion Week[cite: 1].

Indirect (Institutional Frameworks): State institutions including the Ministry of Culture, INSAAC, and UNESCO partnerships[cite: 1]. Their involvement to date has been reactive and event-based rather than architectural[cite: 1].

Core Constraints:
• Institutional Risks: Lack of centralized policy or domestic protectionism[cite: 1].
• Material Value-Chain Risks: High labor times vs. fast commercial imports[cite: 1].
• Interpretive Risks: Binary trap between rigid "authenticity" and Eurocentric baselines[cite: 1].
